Digital Scanline Framework

A digital scanline framework for complex rock-wall structure recognition

The project uses digital scanlines as the core organizing framework to transform complex rock-wall surfaces into traceable, recognizable, reconstructable, and quantifiable structural information for hazard-block volume estimation and field mitigation support.

Core Idea

Use the stability of 2D profiles to understand and compute the complexity of 3D geological bodies.

Joints, fractures, cavities, and overhanging blocks are spatially intertwined in steep rock walls. Direct recognition in 3D often faces occlusion, fragmentation, unordered paths, and inconsistent scales, which also increases the difficulty of algorithm design, maintenance, and future development.

This project frames the method as a Digital Scanline Framework: a dense and adjustable set of digital scanlines is arranged along a fitted baseline on the 3D rock-wall model, converting complex spatial structures into profile-based recognition problems; detected 2D profile features are then organized across neighboring profiles and mapped back into 3D for reconstruction and volume estimation.

The mathematical idea is simple but important: the rock mass is continuous, while scanlines are discrete observations. By controlling sampling density, discrete observations can represent continuous structures at a chosen scale, balancing geological interpretation, algorithmic stability, and engineering usability.

Workflow

Why convert a 3D problem into a sequence of 2D profile problems?

Lower cognitive complexity

2D profiles convert occluded and intertwined 3D structures into continuous curves that are easier to inspect, understand, and validate.

Lower algorithmic complexity

Path tracing, direction estimation, and local geometric rules are more stable in 2D, making the system easier to design, debug, maintain, and extend.

Approximate continuity

Dense adjustable scanline sampling lets discrete profiles represent continuous rock-mass behavior at the intended analysis scale.

01

3D model input

Use a triangular mesh or point cloud to preserve spatial geometry and local surface variation.

02

Baseline fitting and sampling control

Fit a curved baseline and control scanline density to match the target analysis scale.

03

Digital scanline slicing

Generate continuous profile planes along the baseline as 2D structural observation windows.

04

2D feature detection

Trace profile paths, compute local directions, and detect overhangs, cavities, and protrusions.

05

Cross-profile grouping

Connect corresponding features across neighboring profiles into clusterable structural groups.

06

3D back-projection reconstruction

Project detected 2D features back into 3D as blocks, faces, voxels, and density fields.

Application Result

From detected structures to potential hazard-block volume estimation.

The value of structural recognition is not only visualization. Detected structural groups can be reconstructed as 3D candidate blocks, and their volumes can be estimated. Larger potential hazard blocks can then be prioritized for field verification, clearing, anchoring, or protective-net planning.

The figure shows the ten largest reconstructed candidate blocks by volume, turning geometric extraction into engineering-oriented quantitative evidence.

Spatial Distribution Analysis

Density maps of different block-size ranges reveal structural zoning.

Once structural units are detected and quantified, they can be grouped by size and analyzed through 3D kernel density estimation. Dense small-block regions may indicate strongly weathered, fractured, or fragmented zones, while concentrations of larger blocks may suggest more critical instability targets.

This connects geometric recognition, spatial statistics, and engineering interpretation, making the toolkit useful not only as a detector but also as a support tool for hazard zoning and field investigation planning.

Vision

Toward a more precise, broader, and more integrated digital geology framework.

Direction 1

Finer recognition of complex structures

Future versions may introduce foundation-model or learning-based components for key generalization-heavy steps, while preserving stable geometric constraints through the code framework.

Direction 2

Broader geological applications

The workflow can expand from overhanging-block recognition to rock-mass structure extraction, fracture and discontinuity representation, hazard zoning, and field-survey support.

Direction 3

2D framework and 3D model fusion

2D profiles are powerful for stable detection; 3D models are powerful for spatial expression. A key goal is to combine their strengths for better geological interpretation.
